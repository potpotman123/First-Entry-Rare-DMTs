What the prior analysis changes about our original question

We still do not have a defensible basis for claiming that analysts systematically underforecast first rare-disease DMT revenue. The prior analysis makes a mechanism-based, case-by-case investigation substantially more actionable. It supplies dated report leads, a reproduced model table, and both favorable and unfavorable examples.

The useful next question is: when did analysts underestimate the commercially reachable, treatment-responsive patient pool or paid treatment exposure, and are the same omissions present in CMP-002 forecasts? That question can support an independent forecast and a defensible investor argument. A broad claim that “rare-disease analysts are conservative” cannot yet do either.

This conclusion does not establish unbiased forecasting. The strict matched three- and five-year samples remain empty because original annual forecast curves, risk handling and scope are not fully recoverable from the supplied materials. The new evidence supports targeted tests, not a numerical bias premium.

Main new source: user-supplied 16-page prior analysis, especially pp.3–5 and 7–16. The deck itself concludes that no consistent directional trend emerged. The audit distinguishes the compiler’s summaries from the underlying reports, which have not been supplied.

What is genuinely useful in the prior work

Its strongest contribution is a set of candidate mechanisms rather than a pooled forecasting statistic. Strensiq and Crysvita raise the question of whether adults or less obvious patients were omitted. Givlaari supplies the opposite hypothesis: a large undiagnosed pool may be hard to find or convert into treatment. Skyclarys raises the transition from known patients at specialist centers to a dispersed community population. Vyjuvek raises the distinction between patient penetration and continuing paid treatment. SMA separates disease-market demand from the share captured by a particular modality.

The deck explicitly says its prelaunch estimates are not probability adjusted. That is useful evidence and is preserved as a compiler declaration. It is not uniformly verified at the model level: the Evrysdi appendix mentions 90% approval probability in one row. We should obtain the underlying equation rather than either discard the declaration or divide every number by a guessed PoS.

Five products are added to our earlier screening ledger: Crysvita, Vyjuvek, Givlaari, Zolgensma and Evrysdi. Zolgensma and Evrysdi remain fast-follower comparators, not independent first-DMT launches. The integrated ledger now contains 53 candidate/control entries; it is still not an exhaustive approval census.

These tracks should all be retained, but they cannot be combined into one “beat rate.” Crysvita’s stated 2025 sales of $1.6bn are inside its displayed $1.2–1.8bn old peak range. Givlaari’s revised $0.6bn peak is also inside its displayed $0.5–1.5bn range. Neither establishes a uniform direction across analysts. Likewise, current Vyjuvek sales below a future peak estimate do not establish a miss.

Ranges hide important information: the number of analysts, the center of the distribution, exact vintage, scope, and whether the same analyst changed their view. Comparing range midpoints would introduce assumptions rather than recover a historical consensus.

The Strensiq model excerpt on p.8 is dated January 19, 2016, after approval. It models patients under 18 despite acknowledging the broad label, and gives $1.2bn worldwide sales for 2025. This directly supports an omitted-population hypothesis for that particular model. It does not, by itself, prove the preapproval consensus omitted the same patients or quantify their contribution to the subsequent beat.

The relevant distinction is age at disease onset versus age when treated. Strensiq’s original indication covers perinatal/infantile- and juvenile-onset disease; adults with pediatric-onset disease can fit that definition. This is different from assuming all adult-onset HPP is eligible. Its weight-based dosing and survival effects also mean adult expansion is not a clean revenue-per-patient analogue for an intrathecal ASO.

Return to CMP-002 and the original research question

Do preapproval forecasts systematically underestimate approval-conditional revenue for first rare-disease DMTs? If so, under which observable conditions, and are those conditions absent from the current CMP-002 forecast?

The current answer is “not established overall; specific omissions are plausible and testable.” The prior deck supports the move toward case-by-case analysis that was already anticipated in the original task. It does not support reversing the burden of proof and requiring every analogue to explain why CAMP is undervalued.

Needham already assumes a 2032 launch, diagnosis growth from 15% to 50%, 50% penetration of diagnosed patients, a 75% adherence/persistence factor, and approximately $2.1bn conditional worldwide peak revenue. It separately applies 25% PoS in valuation. A diagnosis-expansion narrative therefore needs to show that the assumed rate, timing or eligible denominator is too conservative—not merely that diagnosis can rise after a treatment becomes available.

Clinical breadth is the gating uncertainty. A seizure-only profile, a broad functional-benefit profile, and a benefit concentrated in younger patients imply different adoption and persistence. The historical examples cannot establish that CMP-002 will reverse established adult neurodevelopmental impairment. This remains a product-profile scenario until supported by human evidence.

The next collection should be narrow and decisive

First retrieve the exact Credit Suisse September 2, 2014 Strensiq report and January 19, 2016 model; the BofA September 30, 2014 note is necessary to reconcile the peak range. Next obtain the January 19, 2016 Spinraza report, which fits the intended preapproval vintage, and the original Crysvita model assumptions behind pediatric versus adult coverage. Givlaari and Skyclarys provide the disconfirming patient-finding and continuation cases. The Jefferies Zolgensma and Bernstein Evrysdi originals resolve the major scope/PoS issues without expanding into a whole SMA valuation project.

For each recovered model, extract the original annual curve and its patient, pricing, territory, label and risk assumptions. Match the same periods and scope to actuals. Preserve peak revisions as a separate outcome. Only then compute drug-level signed cumulative errors; no bias coefficient enters the CMP-002 model yet.

A stronger investor claim, if those checks succeed, would identify a specific omitted eligible population, underestimated conversion rate or durable treatment exposure in a named model, supported by evidence relevant to CMP-002. That is more defensible than a general assertion about sell-side pessimism.
