CAMP4 PLATFORM BACKGROUND
CAMP Background and the RAP Platform: ASO-mediated gene upregulation

CAMP’s RAP platform utilizes ASOs to upregulate an endogenous gene without altering DNA. The approach works through regulatory
RNA (regRNAs), a class of RNA that play an important role in controlling gene expression by acting locally on transcription factors at
the enhancer/promoter level. CAMP maps the regRNA that is controlling a target gene of interest and designs an ASO that binds to the
regRNA, which amplifies gene expression. Mechanistically, the ASO binds to the target regRNA and displaces transcriptional repressor
proteins, which drives more transcription and ultimately the production of more protein.

regRNAs are short-lived and relatively scarce, so CAMP built a proprietary regRNA capture method to identify them and has assembled a
proprietary catalog of regRNA sequences across diverse cell and tissue types that could be amenable to therapeutic intervention. The use
of ASOs is a clinically-validated therapeutic modality, with established chemical composition and delivery methods. CAMP’s approach to
target regRNAs allow for a programmable, tunable, and re-dosable way to upregulate a protein of interest, while avoiding the drawbacks
and issues of delivery, immunogenicity, and safety associated with certain gene therapy or gene editing approaches.

SYNGAP1 BACKGROUND, UNMET NEED, AND CAMP'S LEAD DRUG CMP-002
SYNGAP1 Background

SYNGAP1-related   disorder   (abbr.   SYNGAP1)   is   a   monogenic   developmental   and   epileptic   encephalopathy   (DEE)   caused   by
haploinsufficiency of the SYNGAP1 gene (one mutated SYNGAP1 gene and one unmutated functioning copy), which yields SYNGAP1
protein ~50% of normal levels. The large majority are de novo mutations, with truncating (loss-of-function) variants predominating.

        Epidemiology:  Prevalence/Incidence: CAMP estimates the prevalence is ~11K in the US and ~10K in the EU4 + UK. Based on
        a literature review using incidence rates and adjusted for population, we arrive at similar estimates that are in line with CAMP.
        For example, prevalence and incidence rates in the literature vary, but generally range from 1-4 per 10K; or ~6 per 100K births.
        SYNGAP1 is estimated to represent 0.5-1% of all US cases of intellectual and development disability in children (some sources

        Symptoms:  The most common symptoms are (% of estimated patients occurring): developmental delay / intellectual disability
        (100%); generalized epilepsy (~84%); and autism and other behavioral abnormalities (<50%); other common symptoms include
        hypotonia, sleep disturbances, and feeding issues. Developmental delay precedes seizure onset in the vast majority (~95%) of
        patients, and initial symptoms typically start in infancy. Seizure onset then follows at a median age of ~3 years. No robust genotype–
        phenotype correlations have been established.

        Diagnosis:  A genetic test is required to confirm a diagnosis. Because early symptoms (developmental delay, hypotonia, autism)
        overlap with other neurodevelopmental disorders, the journey from onset to diagnosis often spans several years. The median age
        of genetic diagnosis is ~3.8 years (IQR 2.7-6.8; Citizen Health + CHOP natural-history cohorts), consistent with the ~4-year median
        (range 2-7) in Wiltrout 2025.  Diagnosis rates remain low - we model ~15-20% based on our literature/public-resource review,
        and the ~1,871 census against prevalence implies the global rate may be even lower.  SYNGAP1 is not included in standard
        newborn screening panels, and most newborns are not screened for genetic disease at birth, so like most rare monogenic disorders
        the majority of cases go unidentified.

        Treatment:  There are no approved or disease-modifying therapies, and no there is nothing on the market that addresses the
        underlying genetic cause. Since most patients have epilepsy, anti-seizure medications are used off-label as front-line therapy
        (e.g. Epidiolex, valproate, clobazam, levetiracetam, and lamotrigine are most commonly used), and polypharmacy is common.
        However, up to 50% of SYNGAP-1 related epilepsies are drug-resistant. In drug-resistant cases, patients may undergo vagus nerve
        stimulation or a strict ketogenic diet. Melatonin and perampanel are used in some patients with sleep disturbances, and behavioral
        and psychiatric drugs (e.g. risperidone, aripiprazole) are used in some patients to help manage the symptoms of autism and
        behavioral issues.

        Clinical endpoints:  There are no approved or regulatory validated endpoints for SYNGAP1. In the Phase 1/2 trial, CAMP plans to
        evaluate a range of relevant measures including seizures, Bayley-4, Vineland-3, Aberrant Behavior Checklist, 2nd edition (ABC-2),
        Observer-Reported Communication Ability Measure (ORCA), Gross Motor Function Measure (GMFM), clinician global impression
        (CGI-S/CGI-I), and patient-reported outcome for sleep, behavior, and quality of life (QoL).

WHAT ARE THE UNMET NEEDS IN SYNGAP1-RELATED DISORDER?
Improved diagnosis: The average time to diagnosis is ~5 years from the first symptom. Diagnosis rates are relatively low and are
estimated to be ~15-20% of the total prevalence. Improvements in time to diagnosis and diagnosis rates are needed, and we feel that these
would likely dramatically improve if a disease-modifying therapy were available.

Better seizure control that anti-seizure medications (ASMs) can’t deliver: ~85% of patients have epilepsy, and these patients often
present with multiple types of seizures that are commonly resistant to the standard regimen of ASMs. Seizure burden/control is one of
the most important areas of unmet need in SYNGAP1.

No disease-modifying therapies available: There are no approved drugs for SYNGAP1. SoC drugs are used to manage symptoms and
include off-label use of anti-seizure medications, cannabinoids, and sleep medications, but none of these options address the genetic
cause (haploinsufficiency) of the disease. CMP-002 is being positioned as the first disease-modifying therapy.

Therapies that improve cognition / intellectual disability: ~100% of SYNGAP1 patients have an intellectual disability, and there is
currently no therapy that improves this trajectory. SoC in this area is limited to behavorial/educational/speech therapy. Along with
seizures, this is one of the most important areas of unmet need.

CMP-002 is an antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) designed to bind to a specific regulatory RNA sequence, thereby increasing SYNGAP1
gene expression and protein production. By restoring these protein levels closer to wild-type, the therapy ultimately aims to
normalize synaptic function to improve symptoms and potentially modify the disease in patients with SYNGAP1-related disorders.

CMP-002: PRECLINICAL DATA SUMMARY
CMP-002 shows target engagement and proof-of-mechanism in human cells and translation in a “humanized” SYNGAP1 mouse
model (Figure 5)

Figure 5 shows the data for an in vitro experiment in SYNGAP1 patient iPSC-derived neurons. The graph plots the effect of increasing
CMP-002 concentrations (0 to 200 nM) or a nontargeting ASO control (“NTC”) on SYNGAP1 mRNA levels. The three curves represent:
1) a familial control + NTC (grey line); 2) SYNGAP1 patient neurons + NTC (purple line); and 3) SYNGAP1 patient neurons + CMP-002
(teal line). The data are normalized to the familial control neurons + NTC (grey line), so SYNGAP1 mRNA levels are displayed as x-fold
in reference to the control.

Since the familial (wild-type) control (grey line) represents the wild-type SYNGAP1 mRNA levels, by definition it stays at 1.0x across the
entire dose range. In the SYNGAP1 patient neurons + NTC (purple line), the SYNGAP1 mRNA levels remain ~50% below the control (wild-
type levels), and represents the expected haploinsufficiency (~50% expression levels) baseline. In the SYNGAP1 patient neurons + CMP-002

CMP-002 Data in Humanized Mouse Model Restores SYNGAP protein to near wild-type levels. (Figure 6)

In a separate set of in vivo experiments, CAMP evaluated CMP-002 in a humanized SYNGAP1 mouse model, to determine whether
SYNGAP1 protein levels could be increased with treatment. The data below show ICV dosing at P21 in a humanized SYNGAP1 mouse
model and the data readout is SYNGAP1 protein, normalized to a homozygous control.

The figure shows in vivo data from a humanized mouse showing SYNGAP1 protein levels normalized to a homozygous control (grey bar).
The bars to the right of the control show data in haploinsufficient mice, with the artificial CSF (aCSF) control (purple bar) showing the
expected ~50% SYNGAP1 protein expression relative to the homozygous control (grey). Across the three ascending doses (25 / 50 / 100
ug) of CMP-002 in haploinsufficient mice, the SYNGAP1 protein levels start at ~50% and increase in a dose-dependent fashion to ~75% of
the homozygous control group (which approximates the wild-type levels).

        Needham takeaways: We note there are the usual preclinical caveats to the data, including different readouts across data sets
        (in vitro mRNA levels vs. protein levels in a mouse), so not a direct comparison within a single system. That said, we think the
        takeaways are that CMP-002 increased SYNGAP1 protein to wild-type levels in a dose-dependent manner, and the data suggests
        that CMP-002 target engagement/activity in vitro translates to the transcription level (mRNA) and converts to functional protein in
        vivo. We note that in the mouse model, the magnitude of SYNGAP1 protein expression is roughly calibrated to the therapeutically
        relevant window, in that the dose levels shown restore SYNGAP1 protein to levels above the haploinsufficent baseline but do not
        exceed the wild-type levels, which could be problematic from a safety/toxicity perspective.

Intrathecal administration of CMP-002 in NHPs leads to broad, dose-dependent distribution across disease-relevant brain regions
and a ~1.3-1.6x increase in SYNGAP1 protein levels. (Figure 7)

Study design: CMP-002 was administered via intrathecal injection (low/mid/high dose) Q2W x 3 doses to healthy, wild-type cynomolgus
monkeys (n=4 per group), plus a control group (data are normalized to aCSF). Necropsy was performed 2 weeks after last dose.

Figure 7 (left) shows CMP-002 concentration (y-axis; normalized on a 0-100 scale) across six regions of the brain: frontal cortex, temporal
cortex, parietal cortex, motor cortex, occipital cortex, and hippocampus. The exact dose levels are not disclosed, but are low / mid / high.
The data show a dose-dependent increase in CMP-002 concentration across all of the listed brain regions, with no region below the ~50
range, which suggests CMP-002 is reaching all six of the listed regions.

PD follows the PK effect - dose-dependent increases in SYNGAP1 protein. In Figure 7 (right), the data shows SYNGAP1 protein levels
from the frontal cortex (highest CMP-002 concentration at high dose) and the hippocampus (lowest CMP-002 concentration at high dose).
SYNGAP1 protein levels increase in a dose-dependent manner in both the frontal cortex and the hippocampus, and from our estimation
of the graph, we see a ~30-60% increase in SYNGAP protein levels at the high dose. This tracks well with the PK data and CMP-002 levels
shown in the figure on the right.

Collectively, this data supports our confidence in the PK/PD correlation and the ability of CMP-002 to get into clinically relevant regions of
the brain. The magnitude of the protein increase (~30-60%) is also clinically relevant if one assumes a similar increase in haploinsufficient
SYNGAP1 protein levels of ~50% of normal at baseline, that would put near the wild-type or normal levels, which also supports the mouse
model data discussed above.

Our key takeaways from the CAMP analyst day on 9/28/26 as it pertains to the CMP-002 Phase 1/2 Trial in SYNGAP1:



■      Seizure reduction as a potential registrational endpoint. CAMP mgmt noted that as a 1st-in-disease program (no approved drugs
    for SYNGAP1) they can't yet define a meaningful threshold for success; regulatory discussions have centered around having reliable
    and validated ways to assess changes across multiple methods rather than agreeing on a specific number (e.g. seizure reduction %).
    Mgmt has engaged regulators globally and is intentionally trying to keep them "open-minded"; CAMP mgmt declined to pre-define/
    pre-commit to clinical endpoints, emphasizing reliable, validated methodologies so that any observed movement is credible.



■      Dose-response expectations. Mgmt has not yet disclosed the dose levels but noted that all three dose levels are "within the expected
    therapeutic range", framing the initial low starting dose as a safety-oriented, peer-consistent approach.



■      Natural history study comparison. Mgmt acknowledged natural history (NH) visit cadence (often ~6-monthly) is less frequent
    than the visits on the Phase 1/2 ASCEND trial but noted NH assesses a broader window; they highlighted the ≥4-week baseline and
    the internal sham control for comparison. The Phase 1/2 ASCEND trial is not powered for statistical significance vs. control, but
    numerical comparisons can be made.



■      Timing of data disclosure - 1H28 initial topline disclosure is a conservative base case. Mgmt's goal is to deliver a high-quality trial
    with high confidence in the results that also provides development pathway optionality going forward. They will not be reviewing data
    on a streaming/routine basis to protect the double-blind integrity of the trial (important given caregiver/clinician-rated assessments).
    Certain (undisclosed) pre-specified review points exist but interim commentary or disclosure is not promised. Mgmt noted there

PIPELINE BEYOND SYNGAP1

■      SHANK3  (Phelan-McDermid  Syndrome):  SHANK3  is  a  post-synaptic  scaffolding  protein  and  haploinsufficiency  presents  a

    more on primate data (plus related mouse models) as the humanized-mouse model is less established than SYNGAP1's.




■      Two additional brain haploinsufficiency programs are expected to be disclosed over the next year, which mgmt has framed as
    franchise-leveraging shared brain targets, with intrathecal delivery and a haploinsufficiency mechanism.
